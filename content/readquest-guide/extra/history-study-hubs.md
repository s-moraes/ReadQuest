# From Leitner Boxes to Modern Study Hubs

The human brain is prone to rapid forgetting if new information is not periodically reinforced. Over the last century, learning science has transformed from physical index cards into intelligent digital systems.

---

## The Leitner System (1970s)

In 1972, German science journalist **Sebastian Leitner** introduced the *Leitner Box*—a physical cardboard container divided into 5 compartment slots:

```text
[ Box 1: Daily ] -> [ Box 2: Every 3 Days ] -> [ Box 3: Weekly ] -> [ Box 4: Mastered ]
      ^                                               |
      +------------ On Failure Return to Box 1 -------+
```

Cards answered correctly advanced to the next compartment, while cards answered incorrectly immediately fell back to Box 1. This simple heuristic ensured students spent the majority of their time reviewing difficult material rather than wasting effort on mastered concepts.

---

## The Digital Renaissance: SuperMemo & Anki (1980s–2000s)

In the late 1980s, **Piotr Woźniak** formalized mathematical models of human memory decay, creating the **SM-2 spaced repetition algorithm**. This algorithm became the foundation for software like SuperMemo and Anki.

While flashcards proved unbeatable for vocabulary acquisition, they struggled to communicate complex engineering systems:
- System design cannot easily be reduced to a 5-word flashcard.
- Software engineering requires reading architectural context, reviewing code snippets, and inspecting trade-off matrices.

---

## The ReadQuest Hybrid Philosophy

ReadQuest synthesizes deep contextual reading with active recall:

1. **Context First:** Students read structured chapters with syntax-highlighted code and architectural diagrams.
2. **Immediate Active Recall:** Timed assessments force the brain to retrieve knowledge right after reading.
3. **Progressive Gating:** Advanced topics and case studies unlock only after proving mastery over foundational concepts.
